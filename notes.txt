a visitor makes a purchase?

FIGURE 5 — CONFUSION MATRIX

Finding:
The balanced logistic regression correctly identified 255 of the
382 actual purchasing sessions, while missing 127 purchasers.

However, the model also incorrectly classified 720 non-purchasing
sessions as purchases.

Interpretation:
The balanced model is much better at identifying actual purchasers
than the original logistic regression. It detected 66.75% of
purchasing sessions, compared with only 3.93% for the initial model.

The trade-off is a large number of false positives. The model is
therefore better suited to situations where missing a potential
purchaser is more costly than incorrectly targeting a non-purchaser.

Business Takeaway:
The model could potentially support relatively low-cost actions,
such as website personalization or marketing prompts, where false
positives are acceptable. It would be less appropriate for costly
interventions because many predicted purchasers do not actually buy.

FIGURE 6 — PRECISION-RECALL CURVE

Finding:
The precision-recall curve shows a trade-off between identifying
more purchasing sessions and maintaining precise predictions.

As recall increases, precision generally decreases. This means
the model can identify a larger share of actual purchasers only
by also incorrectly flagging more non-purchasing sessions.

The final model's average precision is 30.63%, compared with an
overall purchase rate of 15.47%.

Business Interpretation:
The model provides some ability to distinguish purchasing sessions,
but the appropriate classification threshold depends on the
business cost of false positives compared with missed purchasers.

5. MODEL RESULTS
----------------

Accuracy:          84.47%

Average Precision: 30.76%

Confusion Matrix:
True Negatives:  2,068

Interpretation:
The initial logistic regression performed poorly at identifying
purchasing sessions.

Although its overall accuracy was 84.47%, this was approximately
the same as an always-no-purchase baseline. The model correctly
identified only 15 of 382 actual purchasing sessions, resulting
in recall of only 3.93%.

The model therefore favored the majority non-purchase class.
Accuracy alone makes the model appear much stronger than it
actually is for detecting purchases.

6. FIVE-FOLD CROSS-VALIDATION
-----------------------------

Settings compared:
- Logistic regression with no class weighting
- Logistic regression with balanced class weighting

Cross-validation:
- 5 folds
- Optimization metric: F1 score
- Performed using training data only

Best setting:
class_weight = "balanced"

Best cross-validation F1 score:
0.3697

Interpretation:
Five-fold cross-validation selected balanced class weighting.
This gives greater importance to the less common purchase class
and produced a better balance between precision and recall than
the standard logistic regression.

The test set was not used to select this setting.

7. FINAL LOGISTIC REGRESSION RESULTS
-----------------------
Five-fold cross-validation selected:
class_weight = "balanced"

Final Test Results:
Accuracy:          65.65%
Precision:         26.15%
Recall:            66.75%
F1 Score:          37.58%
Average Precision: 30.63%

Always-No-Purchase Baseline Accuracy:
84.51%

Final Confusion Matrix:
True Negatives:  1,364
False Positives:   720
False Negatives:   127
True Positives:    255

MAIN FINDING:
Balancing the classes substantially improved the model's ability
to detect purchasing sessions.

The initial logistic regression identified only 15 of 382 actual
purchasing sessions, producing recall of 3.93%.

The balanced logistic regression identified 255 of 382 actual
purchasing sessions, increasing recall to 66.75%.

TRADE-OFF:
The improvement in recall came at the cost of lower precision and
accuracy. The balanced model produced 720 false positives and had
precision of 26.15%.

Although the always-no-purchase baseline achieves higher accuracy
(84.51%), it identifies zero purchasing sessions. Therefore,
accuracy alone is not an appropriate measure of usefulness for
this business problem.

BUSINESS INTERPRETATION:
The balanced model may be useful when the cost of targeting a
false positive is relatively low and the business places greater
value on identifying more potential purchasers.

However, the model's relatively low precision means it should not
be treated as a highly reliable purchase prediction system.

LLM used:

Purpose:
We asked the LLM to interpret the trade-off between our initial
and balanced logistic regression and challenge our interpretation
of the improved recall.

Key insight:
The LLM identified that although recall increased dramatically
from 3.93% to 66.75%, average precision remained nearly unchanged
(30.76% vs. 30.63%).

This suggests that balanced class weighting primarily changed the
model's classification behavior rather than dramatically improving
its underlying ability to rank purchasers above non-purchasers.

How it affected our analysis:
We changed our interpretation from simply stating that the
balanced model was "better" to describing the specific trade-off
between detecting purchasers and generating false positives.

The LLM also suggested that the usefulness of the model depends
on the business cost of false positives and false negatives.

Verification:
We verified all performance values against the metrics calculated
directly in Python and checked the confusion matrices.

The initial model identified 15 of 382 purchasers.
The balanced model identified 255 of 382 purchasers.

Average precision changed only from 30.76% to 30.63%, confirming
the LLM's observation that the improvement was mainly in the
classification trade-off rather than overall ranking performance.
